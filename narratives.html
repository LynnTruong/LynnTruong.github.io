Lynn Truong Narratives

random little stories from a copywriter's life

Some stories about words, work, and the moments that taught me how to write better. Nothing polished. Just real.

LT

Narrative 01
"You're writing like someone looking in from outside, not someone looking out from inside."

The first campaign I ever wrote in Vietnamese

I was working in Sài Gòn at the time, sitting in a small office on the second floor, right next to the Chairman's room. My title was Assistant to the Chairman, while also overseeing the sales and marketing department. I was given a deadline for a brand communications campaign tied to an upcoming client conference: a press release, a strategy plan, and a tagline for the event.

I brought all my enthusiasm, and a vocabulary full of "modern, international" words I'd picked up as a student in Singapore. So I wrote what I thought modern meant: long sentences, formal words, the kind of language I'd seen in English magazine ads translated word for word.

In under three days, I finished it and brought it in to the Chairman. Bác (that's how I addressed him, since he allowed it, and we were close) was a calm man, gentle in appearance, but very firm in how he worked. I didn't think he'd be familiar with professional marketing language, so part of me felt confident a Singapore graduate like myself wouldn't get criticized.

He read it slowly, in silence, while I sat there sweating more than the Sài Gòn heat could explain. Then he looked up and said one sentence I've never forgotten: "You're writing like someone looking in from outside, not someone looking out from inside."

I didn't fully understand it then. I do now. That sentence has quietly guided every piece of Vietnamese copy I've written since.

Memories of time working for Công Thành Group (congthanhgroup.com).

Narrative 02
"Cô Liên đây! An Tâm Con Hỉ!"
Six words. Three days.

What 20 years in marketing taught me about silence

Early in my career, I thought good copy meant filling every inch of space. More words meant more value, more persuasion, more reasons to buy. I wrote long. I wrote "thorough." Clients nodded politely and rarely remembered a word of it.

Years later, working on the Cô Liên Beef Shop project, I spent three days on a single line: "Cô Liên đây! An Tâm Con Hỉ!" Six words. My early career self would have written a paragraph about thirty years of family tradition, quality ingredients, and trust. Instead, six words carried all of it, because they sounded like something an actual person would say to you in a market in Đà Nẵng.

It took longer to write six words than it would have taken to write sixty. That's the part nobody tells you about good copywriting. The shortest lines are usually the ones that took the longest to find.

Narrative 03
"Này, cái này ngon lắm, thử đi."
Hey, this one's really good, try it.

Why I still write like I'm talking to my neighbor

I grew up in a neighborhood where everyone knew everyone's business. Aunties leaning over balconies, kids running between houses, someone always calling out from a window to ask if you'd eaten yet. It wasn't polished. It was warm, direct, and a little nosy in the best way.

That's still how I write Vietnamese copy today. When a brand asks me to write "Dear valued customer, we are pleased to introduce," I hear my old neighborhood telling me that's not how anyone actually talks. Instead I write something closer to: "Này, cái này ngon lắm, thử đi." Hey, this one's really good, try it.

Clients sometimes hesitate at first. It feels too casual, too simple. Then the engagement numbers come in, and they understand: people don't trust brands that sound like brands. They trust people who sound like people. That's the whole job, really. Staying that person, even while writing for a company.
